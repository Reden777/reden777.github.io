Gemini: Elitism did not appear in computer science as a single event; rather, it evolved in distinct waves as computing transitioned from military engineering into an academic discipline and a global industry. 

Ironically, the **earliest form of elitism was directed *against* programmers**, before turning inward in the 1960s to become the cultural and academic gatekeeping familiar today.

---

### 1. The 1940s–1950s: Hardware Elitism (Programming as "Clerical Work")
The earliest hierarchy in digital computing emerged during World War II and the immediate post-war period (such as the development of the ENIAC in 1945). At that time, computer science did not yet exist as an independent field. 

* **The Hierarchy:** Prestigious scientific work was defined strictly as **mathematical theory and hardware electrical engineering**. Designing vacuum-tube circuits and logical architectures was considered "serious" science done by male researchers (such as J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly).
* **The "Coders":** In contrast, the actual programming—setting switches, plugging cables, and translating mathematical formulas into machine steps—was dismissed as low-status, mechanical clerical labor. Because it was considered akin to operating a telephone switchboard or typing, it was assigned almost entirely to women (such as the famous six original ENIAC programmers: Jean Bartik, Betty Holberton, and their peers).
* **Early Resistance to Abstraction:** This hierarchy was so entrenched that when young researchers first developed assemblers to automate the translation of instructions into binary, mathematical luminaries scoffed at it. Most famously, mathematician **John von Neumann** reacted angrily to a student building an assembler, protesting that it was a waste of a valuable scientific computing instrument to use it to do "clerical work."

### 2. The Late 1950s–1960s: The Mainframe "Computer Priesthood"
As computing spread to universities, governments, and corporations via mainframes (like the IBM 704 and System/360), machines were astronomically expensive and fragile. 

* **The Glass House:** Mainframes were locked away in air-conditioned, glass-walled rooms. 
* **The Priesthood:** A specialized caste of operators and systems technicians—who frequently wore white lab coats—controlled direct access to the machines. Ordinary researchers, students, and employees were not permitted to touch the computer; they were forced to submit decks of punched cards through a window and wait hours or days for printouts.
* This institutional gatekeeping became known culturally as the **"Computer Priesthood."** As technologist **Ted Nelson** wrote in his landmark 1974 manifesto *Computer Lib / Dream Machines*: 
  > *"Knowledge is power and so it tends to be hoarded... Experts in any field rarely want people to understand what they do and generally enjoy putting people down. Thus if we say that the use of computers is dominated by a priesthood... it is the control of aspects of society by cliques of insiders."*
  Nelson coined the term **"cybercrud"** to describe how this elite used arcane jargon to make computers seem incomprehensible to ordinary people, thereby preserving their power.

### 3. The 1960s: The "Software Crisis" and the Cult of the Male Genius
By the mid-1960s, the economic value of software skyrocketed, leading to the 1968 NATO Software Engineering Conference and what was termed the **"Software Crisis"** (software projects were consistently over budget, late, and buggy). 

As historian Nathan Ensmenger documents in *The Computer Boys Take Over*, the industry responded by attempting to professionalize and elevate the prestige of programming:
* **The "Black Art":** Programmers rebranded their labor from routine clerical work into an esoteric, creative "black art" that supposedly required rare innate genius.
* **Aptitude Testing:** Companies like IBM and the System Development Corporation (SDC) introduced psychological profiling and aptitude tests to hire programmers. These tests selected heavily for a specific personality type: detached, competitive, mathematically inclined young men.
* **Exclusion:** This newly manufactured elitism systematically edged women out of the field they had helped pioneer, establishing programming as an exclusive, high-salary "boys' club."

### 4. Mid-1960s–1970s: Academic Elitism and Theoretical Purism
As the first independent Computer Science departments were created (Purdue in 1962, Stanford in 1965), academic computer science struggled for respectability within universities. Older fields like pure mathematics and theoretical physics looked down on CS as a vocational "trade school" skill.

To secure academic legitimacy, CS pioneer departments overcompensated by fostering a fierce **theoretical elitism**:
* Academic CS elevated formal mathematical logic, proofs of program correctness, and abstract automata theory, while looking down on practical, real-world software engineering.
* **Edsger W. Dijkstra** became the poster child of this intellectual purism. Dijkstra was notoriously contemptuous of practical or business-oriented programming languages. In 1975, he famously proclaimed:
  > *"It is practically impossible to teach good programming to students that have had a prior exposure to BASIC: as potential programmers they are mentally mutilated beyond hope of regeneration."*  
  *(He similarly declared that teaching COBOL "should be regarded as a criminal offense.")*
* Dijkstra's abrasive intellectual gatekeeping led pioneer Alan Kay to later remark: *"Arrogance in computer science is measured in nano-Dijkstras."*

### 5. The Late 1960s–1980s: Hacker Culture and "Real Programmers"
Outside bureaucratic institutions, hacker culture emerged at labs like MIT, Stanford, and Carnegie Mellon. While early hackers embraced an open, anti-authoritarian "Hacker Ethic" (as chronicled in Steven Levy’s *Hackers*), it quickly generated its own meritocratic elitism:
* Ordinary computer users were derided as **"lusers."**
* The community valorized working in raw assembly, bit-level manipulation, and bare-metal hardware, treating high-level languages and readable code with disdain.
* This attitude was codified in hacker lore such as *The Story of Mel, a Real Programmer* (1983) and Ed Post’s satirical essay *Real Programmers Don't Use Pascal* (1982), which celebrated suffering, sleepless nights, and deliberate obfuscation as badges of computational superiority.

### 1. The 1990s: The Web Influx, "Script Kiddies," and the Cult of RTFM

The 1990s brought two massive shifts: the rise of desktop software and the explosion of the World Wide Web. Traditional developers felt their exclusive territory was being invaded by amateurs, sparking fierce defensive gatekeeping.

* **Language Snobbery ("Real Code" vs. "Toys"):**
  * The divide between compiled systems languages (C, C++, Assembly) and higher-level rapid development environments became a prime battleground.

  * As millions of beginners bought PCs and got dial-up internet, Usenet newsgroups (like `comp.lang.c`) and early Linux/IRC mailing lists adopted an aggressive defensive posture.

  * Eric S. Raymond codified this subculture in his widely read essay *How To Ask Questions The Smart Way* (2001, synthesizing 90s norms). While intended to teach research etiquette, it rationalized public humiliation and caustic dismissiveness under the banner of intellectual rigor.

* **Security Elitism and the "Script Kiddie":**
  * As hacking entered public consciousness in the mid-to-late 90s (around tools like Back Orifice, NetBus, and port scanners), hacker culture split into a strict caste system.
  * True security researchers looked down on anyone who used pre-made software to execute exploits, coining the derogatory term **"script kiddie."** Respect was reserved exclusively for those who wrote raw shellcode, discovered zero-days, or reverse-engineered binaries in x86 assembly.

Here is what programming looks like when elitism is removed from every layer of the practice:

---

### 1. Philosophy: Pragmatism Over Suffering
Elitism fetishizes unnecessary difficulty: managing your own memory, using bare-metal tools, surviving on four hours of sleep, or doing things "from scratch."

* **Value is judged by outcome, not suffering:** A program’s worth is measured by whether it solves a human problem, is reliable, and is maintainable—not by how mathematically arcane or low-level the code is.
* **No "fake" programmers:** A biologist writing messy Python scripts to analyze cancer data, an accountant automating spreadsheets with VBA, and a web designer tweaking CSS are all recognized as people writing software. The artificial divide between "real engineers" and "scripting amateurs" disappears.
* **Tools are tools, not identities:** Choosing a language or editor is treated like choosing a hammer or a screwdriver. The dogmatic tribal wars (Vim vs. Emacs, C vs. Rust, dynamic vs. static typing) are replaced by: *"Does this tool fit this context and this team?"*

### 2. Tooling and Developer Experience (DX): Empathy as a Standard
In an elitist culture, cryptic error messages and obtuse documentation are treated as natural filters to weed out the weak. In a non-elitist culture:

* **Error messages that teach:** The compiler or runtime is viewed as a collaborator, not an adversary. Modern examples of this philosophy already exist:
  * **Elm** and **Rust** pioneered compiler messages that say: *"I found this error on line 42. Did you mean this variable instead? Here is how to fix it."*
* **Documentation is first-class software:** Good documentation is celebrated as an engineering achievement equal to high-throughput algorithms. A beginner asking a basic question isn't told to "RTFM"; instead, the community treats the question as a bug in the documentation that needs fixing.
* **Accessible abstractions:** Visual programming, low-code tools, high-level frameworks, and AI code generation are treated as legitimate leverage, not "cheating."

### 3. Community Culture: Replacing "Brilliant Jerks" with "Force Multipliers"
Historically, organizations tolerated abusive, hostile behavior from high-performing developers under the excuse of "meritocracy." Without elitism:

* **The end of the "10x Developer" myth:** High value is placed not on the individual hero who writes 1,000 lines of inscrutable code alone at night, but on the **"force multiplier"**—the engineer who writes clear code, mentors juniors, reviews pull requests constructively, and makes the other nine people on the team better.
* **Blameless engineering:** When an outage occurs, the focus is on systemic failure, human factors, and process design, rather than publicly humiliating the engineer who pushed the bad commit.
* **Radical psychological safety:** Developers feel comfortable asking: *"I don't understand how this works, can someone explain it?"* without fear of being labeled incompetent. The impulse to perform hyper-intellectual competence is eliminated.

Gemini: Logo and Smalltalk are deeply intertwined: **Logo was the direct intellectual catalyst for the creation of Smalltalk.** 

Both systems were built on the radical premise that computers should not merely be calculators for military and corporate bureaucracies, but **interactive, creative media for children and human thinking**. Their relationship spans historical origins, shared cognitive philosophy, technical mechanics, and a direct evolutionary lineage that eventually produced modern visual programming languages like Scratch.

### 1. The 1968 Epiphany: How Logo Inspired Smalltalk
In 1968, a young computer scientist named **Alan Kay** visited the MIT AI Lab, where mathematician and educator **Seymour Papert** (along with Wally Feurzeig and Cynthia Solomon) had recently created **Logo**. 

At MIT, Kay witnessed something that shocked the computing establishment of the 1960s: elementary school children writing code to draw shapes and solve mathematical problems using the Logo "Turtle." At a time when computers were multi-million-dollar mainframes locked in glass rooms, seeing ten-year-olds interact directly with a machine was a revelation.

As Kay later recounted, this visit completely changed the trajectory of his career. He realized that computing was destined to be a personal, expressive medium—as fundamental as reading and writing. On his flight home from visiting Papert, Kay sketched the concept for the **Dynabook**: an interactive, notebook-sized personal computer designed for "children of all ages."

---

### 2. The Dynabook and Xerox PARC: Smalltalk as Logo’s Successor
When Alan Kay joined the legendary **Xerox PARC** (Palo Alto Research Center) in the early 1970s, he formed the **Learning Research Group (LRG)** alongside pioneers like Dan Ingalls and Adele Goldberg. Their mission was to build the Dynabook.

To run on this theoretical children's computer, they needed an operating system and language. Kay felt that while Logo was brilliant, it had limitations—namely, that children eventually hit a "ceiling" when trying to build full, complex software systems. 

**Smalltalk was explicitly designed to be the language of the Dynabook.** The goal was to take the intuitive directness of Logo and elevate it into a universal, boundless system where:
* A child could learn it on day one.
* An expert could rewrite the entire operating system, window manager, and compiler using that exact same language.

---

### 3. Shared Educational Philosophy: Piaget and Constructionism
Both Logo and Smalltalk were built around developmental psychology, particularly the work of Swiss psychologist **Jean Piaget**:

* **Seymour Papert and Logo (Constructionism):** Papert had studied directly under Piaget in Geneva. He formulated the theory of **Constructionism**: children learn best when actively engaged in constructing tangible external objects ("objects-to-think-with"). In Logo, the **Turtle** was body-syntonic—children pretended *they* were the turtle turning and walking forward, projecting their physical intuition into mathematical logic.
* **Alan Kay and Smalltalk (Bruner’s Stages of Representation):** Kay expanded Papert’s ideas using psychologist Jerome Bruner’s three modes of cognition:
  1. *Enactive (doing/touching):* Manipulating things with a mouse and graphical user interface.
  2. *Iconic (seeing):* Windows, icons, and menus representing processes.
  3. *Symbolic (abstract):* Writing Smalltalk code.

Both Papert and Kay fiercely opposed the "trade school" view of computer education. Neither wanted to train children to become professional programmers; both wanted to give children a sandbox to construct dynamic simulations, stories, and scientific models.

---

### 4. Technical and Linguistic Parallels
Logo and Smalltalk share key architectural DNA:

* **The Influence of Lisp:** 
  * Logo was created essentially as a "Lisp without parentheses"—a dynamic, interpreted language where code and data are interchangeable.
  * Smalltalk combined the dynamic flexibility and metacircular nature of Lisp with the object/class ideas of Simula 67.
* **Message Passing and "Agents":**
  * In Logo, you issue commands to an active agent: `FORWARD 50`, `RIGHT 90`. The Turtle is an autonomous entity on the screen.
  * Smalltalk universalized this idea into pure Object-Oriented Programming (OOP). In Smalltalk, **everything is an object** that communicates by sending and receiving messages (e.g., `3 + 4` is literally the message `+ 4` sent to the number object `3`). The Logo turtle was a spiritual precursor to the Smalltalk object.
* **Live, Moldable Environments:**
  * Neither system used traditional "compile-link-run" batch cycles. 
  * Both relied on live environments (an interactive prompt / image-based workspace) where code took effect immediately and bugs could be inspected in real time.

---

### 5. Full Circle: Squeak, Etoys, and Scratch
Decades later, the lineages of Logo and Smalltalk converged directly in modern educational computing:

1. **Squeak & Etoys (1990s):** In 1996, Alan Kay, Dan Ingalls, and team created **Squeak**, an open-source revival of Smalltalk-80. Inside Squeak, they built **Etoys**—a scriptable authoring environment for children that explicitly imported Logo's turtle geometry concepts into a modern graphical Smalltalk world.
2. **Scratch (2000s):** **Mitchel Resnick**, a student and close collaborator of Seymour Papert at the MIT Media Lab, sought to create the 21st-century successor to Logo using snap-together blocks. 
3. **The Direct Link:** When Resnick and his team designed the very first version of **Scratch**, they built it entirely inside **Squeak Smalltalk** with guidance from Alan Kay's team. 

In this way, modern block-based programming (Scratch) is quite literally the child of both projects: **Papert’s Logo philosophy realized on top of Kay’s Smalltalk engine.**
